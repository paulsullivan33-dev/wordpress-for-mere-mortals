# The Neighbours

WordPress is one answer to "I want to
publish on my own server". These are the
others this series has covered, and the
honest differences — because the right
choice is mostly about which kind of
work you want to own.

## Ghost

**Ghost** (this series' Ghost guide) is
the closest neighbour: a modern
publishing platform aimed squarely at
newsletters and publications, with
memberships and email built into its
heart rather than bolted on. It runs on
Node.js with its own database, and its
writing experience is more focused than
WordPress's. What it doesn't have is
WordPress's ocean: tens of thousands of
plugins and themes, and an answer
already written for almost every odd
requirement. If you are starting a
publication and nothing unusual is on
the list, Ghost is the sleeker tool; if
you need the site to become three other
things over five years, WordPress's
ecosystem is the argument.

## A wiki: BookStack

**BookStack** (its own guide) is not a
blog at all: it organises writing as
books, chapters and pages, for
documentation a *team* maintains. If
your "site" is really internal knowledge
with an editor group, a wiki's shape
fits better than a post stream — and its
permissions are built for that from the
start, where WordPress's roles
(chapter 10) are built for publishing.

## Static site generators

The PHP guide's world has a quieter
cousin: **static site generators**
(Hugo and friends, and this very series'
MkDocs sites) that turn text files into
finished HTML *once*, on your machine,
and serve the files with no database
and no runtime. They are faster, harder
to break into (there is nothing running
to attack), and cheaper to host — at the
cost of no dashboard, no comments, and
publishing meaning a rebuild. If your
site changes weekly and has one author,
static is often the honest answer; the
moment several non-technical people
must publish from a browser, WordPress
earns its machinery back.

## wordpress.com

The hosted sibling (chapter 1) deserves
a fair line: it sells you the *absence*
of chapters 3, 13 and 16 of this guide.
Updates, backups, security and mail are
their job; in exchange you accept their
prices, their plan gates (custom plugins
and themes live on the higher tiers),
and a landlord. For a site that must
simply *be up*, that trade is often
wise. This guide exists for the other
temperament — the one that wants to see
the machinery — and its lab is, in the
end, the argument: the machinery is
smaller than its reputation.

## And underneath: PHP

Finally, the neighbour below rather
than beside: WordPress is an application
*of* PHP, and the PHP guide covers the
language it is written in — worth
reading when you outgrow configuration
and want to read (or write) a theme's
templates or a small plugin of your own.
